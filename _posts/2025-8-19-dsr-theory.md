Top IS journals emphasize "theory." But how should design science authors use "theory" in their work? The answer is tricky, because traditional approaches to theory building in management research don't fit neatly into a design science context. Outside DSR, canonical approaches to "theory" focus on describing empirical relationships between constructs (Whetten, 1989). But in DSR papers, researchers try to create knowledge by designing new artifacts rather than describing the world (Hevner et al., 2004). If traditional descriptive definitions of "theory" don't seem to apply in a design science context, how should DSR authors fulfill the strong preference for "theory" at MISQ and ISR?

To investigate, I reviewed all self-described design science papers published in the two journals since 2023 and found that authors adopted five broad strategies for meeting the theory requirement in IS. I think there are some key takeaways such as theory may sometimes be optional and the word "theory" is overloaded in DSR. But I'm going to start by just listing the five strategies that I observed.

## Strategies

#### 1. Just don't have theory

A surprising number of DSR authors did not include "theory" in their papers, or at least did not describe their contributions using this terminology.[^2] This strategy was common at ISR, where I counted 8 recent DSR papers that did not mention the word "theory" at all. Examples papers adopting strategy #1 include Zhang et al. (2024), Guo et al. (2024), and Xie et al. (2025). This strategy does not seem to work at MISQ, where I did not find any MISQ papers that did not at least mention some connection with theory.

#### 2. Briefly mention theory

A handful of papers briefly mentioned the importance of theory in design science research without explicitly laying out a theoretical perspective. Examples include Ampel et al. (2024) and Lin et al. (2024) at MISQ and Bossler et al. (2024) at ISR. For example, Ampel et al. (2024) briefly mention that a novel artifact can contribute to knowledge by suggesting a nascent design theory. Strategy #2 is very similar to strategy #1, only there is a brief nod to theory.

#### 3. Offer "theory" as background

A few papers used the word "theory" to describe something that is almost like a literature review. For example, Kim et al. (2023) use the heading "Theory and Motivation" to frame a section of their paper which enumerates desirable traits for explainable AI, provides a 2x2 taxonomy of explainable AI systems, and then digs into limitations of existing approaches in a specific quadrant of the matrix.[^1] Similarly, Faik et al. (2024) offer a section on a "Theoretical Background" which ties together literature on digital inclusion, requirements elicitation, affordances and sensemaking. Later they describe contributions back these literatures as "theoretical contributions." This sense of theory as background is a little different from canonical definitions of "theory" like Whetten (1989) which make much sharper claims about the relationships between specific constructs (e.g., CEO personality → sales).

#### 4. Use a kernel theory

Another strategy was to use a "kernel theory" to guide the development of an artifact. In this context, kernel theories are descriptions of reality which inform prescriptive principles about how to build an artifact. The use of kernel theories in DSR goes back at least to Walls et al. (1992), who articulate some of differences between classical business theory (Dubin, 1978) and theory in a DSR context. Li et al. (2024), Wambsganss et al.(2024) and Liu et al. (2025) each use a kernel theory. For example, Li et al. (2024) use a theory of how people learn ("cognitive load theory") to inform the development of educational software. Note that Liu et al. (2025) describe use of a kernel theory as an example of theory-guided design (Abbasi et al., 2024).

#### 5. Develop a full design theory

Danatzis et al. (2024) propose a full "design theory" by applying a taxonomy from Gregor & Jones (2007). From this perspective, "theory" is a formally-defined, complex structure with eight different sub-components such as constructs and testable propositions. For those trying to sort out the meaning of "theory" in design science research, there is one confusing ripple. In Gregor & Jones the "kernel theories" from Walls et al. (1992) are called "justificatory knowledge." So in a way strategy #5 contains strategy #4. 

## Takeaways

Phew. OK so that lays out the five strategies that DSR researchers use to apply "theory" in their work. I think there are two key takeaways (🎒).

🎒 First, based on this analysis, the word "theory" is a little bit overloaded in contemporary DSR. Theory can mean background knowledge, use of a kernel theory (Walls et al., 1992), or a full-fledged design theory Gregor & Jones, 2007). Because the meaning of "theory" is a little ambiguous within DSR, I think it makes sense for researchers to clearly communicate how they are applying "theory" in their own papers, and how this choice aligns with prior work.

🎒 Second, based on this analysis, "theory" does seem to be optional in design science research, especially at ISR. In the past, I have abandoned projects because I could not see a path towards a theoretical contribution. But in some cases, it may be OK to submit a DSR paper without theory. 

[^1]: The authors also include a system description under the broad header "Theory and Motivation" but I think that may be a formatting mistake.

[^2]: You could argue that papers like Valogianni et al. (2025) offer a kind of theory in that they have a model of the world (people respond to economic incentives). But they don't explicitly use a named theory in their work.
